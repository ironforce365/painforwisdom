## Core Insight
Naming a fear out loud -- even before you are ready to face it -- is itself a micro-act of voluntary discomfort that begins eroding the fear's hold, because avoidance depends on silence and vagueness to survive.

## Story Anchor
Mid-run on muddy trails after rain, Gonzalo catches himself admitting on camera: "I cannot run trails at night... I don't know why, maybe because of the darkness, the wildlife, whatever." Then he adds the key line: "The more I talk about that, the more used I will get, hopefully someday I will go ahead and do a night run on the trails." He is not doing the hard thing yet -- he is naming it, publicly, as a deliberate step toward doing it.

## Framework Connection
This connects to the aMCC Effect: the override muscle does not only grow from physical acts of discomfort -- it begins growing the moment you voluntarily choose to stop avoiding a thought. Each time you name the fear instead of deflecting it, you are doing a micro-rep of override. This is the lowest-friction entry point to growth friction: you do not need gear, a plan, or perfect conditions. You just need honesty about what you are avoiding.

## Practical Application
Before you try to conquer a fear through action, try conquering it through speech first. Say it out loud. Record it. Tell someone. Verbalizing a specific fear (not vaguely "I'm scared" but precisely "I cannot run trails at night because of darkness and wildlife") strips the fear of its abstraction and begins the desensitization process.

## Who It's For
The person who knows exactly what they are afraid of but has never said it out loud. The runner who avoids certain routes, the professional who avoids certain conversations, the parent who avoids certain topics with their kids. They feel the fear daily but keep it formless and unspoken, which lets it stay in control. This is for someone who is not yet ready to act but could be ready to speak.
