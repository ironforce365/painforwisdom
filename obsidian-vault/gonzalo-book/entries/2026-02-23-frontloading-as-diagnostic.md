## Core Insight
Frontloading your hardest effort to the start of the week turns the remaining days into a diagnostic lab -- you learn more about your body and mind from recovering under load than from building toward a peak.

## Story Anchor
Gonzalo flipped his usual Monday routine: instead of finishing the week at a high, he started with a high training load. His explicit purpose was threefold -- learning to read fatigue signals from his body, learning to modulate training load based on those signals, and observing how his mind manufactures or amplifies fatigue throughout the rest of the week. This is not random suffering; it is a structured experiment with himself as the subject.

## Framework Connection
This entry directly illustrates [[strategic-vs-manufactured-suffering]]: the frontloading is not about proving toughness but about creating diagnostic conditions with a clear learning objective. It connects to [[the-three-modes]] because the entire rest-of-week observation requires conscious presence -- you cannot read fatigue signals on cruise control. And it extends [[amcc-effect]] by showing that voluntary discomfort is not just about building override capacity but about building signal literacy -- learning what override actually feels like versus what genuine limit feels like.

## Practical Application
Pick one week per training cycle and frontload your biggest session to Monday. Then spend the rest of the week paying attention: What does real fatigue feel like versus mental fatigue? When does your mind tell you to back off before your body needs to? Journal or voice-note your observations each day. Over time, you build a personal fatigue dictionary -- you learn to distinguish between signals that mean "stop" and signals that mean "your mind is negotiating." This skill transfers directly to race day, where misreading fatigue signals costs you either your health or your potential.

## Who It's For
The endurance athlete (or anyone in a demanding training program) who always builds toward a crescendo but never interrogates what happens after the hard thing. The person who knows how to push but has never learned how to listen. Also relevant for anyone in a high-performance role (engineering sprints, parenting marathons) who defaults to "save the hard stuff for when I'm warmed up" without realizing that starting hard teaches you things that finishing hard never will.

### Angle 1: Perceived vs. actual fatigue (central governor and psychobiological models)
*Why this matters for this entry: The entire premise of frontloading as diagnostic depends on the distinction between fatigue signals the brain generates protectively and fatigue that reflects genuine physiological limit.*

- Chapter 5: "Pain" -- examines how pain signals are interpreted and overridden
- Relevance: Directly addresses when fatigue signals during training are genuine physiological limits or brain-imposed protective estimates
- Verified: yes
